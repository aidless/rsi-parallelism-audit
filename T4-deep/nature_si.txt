Supplementary Methods 
siRNA synthesis 
The agents in AutoDNA autonomously divide the experiment into three phases: double-stranded 
DNA synthesis, transcription, and purification. For the double-stranded DNA synthesis part, a 
double-stranded DNA template was prepared by annealing two complementary single-stranded 
oligonucleotides (each synthesized by Sangon Biotech at 10 µM final concentration; the target 
siRNA sequence is located downstream of the T7 promoter). A 20 µL reaction mixture containing 
1× Annealing Buffer and 10 µM of each oligonucleotide was assembled. The annealing was 
performed in a thermal cycler with the following protocol: 95 °C for 2 min, ramp down to 25 °C 
over 45 min, and hold at 25 °C for 10 min. 
Transcription was performed using the T7 In Vitro Transcription Kit (Takara Bio). A 20 µL 
reaction was assembled by combining 2 µL of 10× Transcription Buffer, 2 µL each of ATP, GTP, 
CTP, and UTP solutions, 0.5 µL of RNase Inhibitor, 2 µL of T7 RNA Polymerase, 6.5 µL of 
nuclease-free water, and the annealed double-stranded DNA template (0.5 µM final 
concentration). The reaction mixture was mixed thoroughly, briefly centrifuged, and incubated at 
42 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the reaction was diluted with 20 µL of nuclease-free water to a 
final volume of 40 µL. 
The transcribed RNA was then purified using the RNA Clean & Concentrator™ MagBead Kit 
(Zymo Research). To the 40 µL transcription reaction, 5 µL of DNase I and 5 µL of DNA 
Digestion Buffer were added and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 150 µL 
of RNA MagBinding Buffer, 15 µL of MagBinding Beads, and 250 µL of 100% isopropanol were 
added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 15 min with gentle agitation. The beads were captured 
using a magnetic rack, and the supernatant was discarded. The bead-bound RNA was washed once 
with 500 µL of RNA Prep Buffer and twice with 500 µL of 80% ethanol. After air-drying the 
beads for 10 min at room temperature, the RNA was eluted in 15 µL of nuclease-free water with 5 
min of mixing. The eluate was separated from the beads using the magnetic rack and collected as 
purified RNA. 
The synthesized siRNA product was analyzed by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis 
and compared to a reference standard (synthesized by Sangon Biotech). The gel was stained with 
CyberGold nucleic acid gel stain (AAT Bioquest) and visualized using a GelDoc Go imaging 
system (Bio-Rad).  
To further validate the sequence accuracy of the synthesized siRNA, we designed a fluorescence 

strand displacement reaction1. The reporter complex consisted of a single-stranded fluorophore 
and a quencher at a 1:1.5 molar ratio (1× = 10 µM). Annealing was performed in a thermal cycler 
under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, slow cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C per 6 s, 
and holding at 4 °C. A 5-nt toehold domain was incorporated to ensure specificity and reaction 
kinetics. 
Dynamic fluorescence measurements were carried out using a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode 
Reader (BioTek) and Corning 96-well black assay plates. The temperature was maintained at 
20 °C throughout the reaction. The TET fluorophore was excited at 510 nm, and emission was 
detected at 540 nm. All components except the input strand were mixed in TE buffer containing 
12.5 mM MgCl₂ at stoichiometric ratios, with the reporter at a final concentration of 250 nM. The 
mixture was aliquoted into a 96-well black plate, and an initial reading was taken to establish a 
baseline. The run was paused to introduce the input strand (150 nM commercially synthesized 
siRNA as a positive control), followed by mixing via shaking. The plate was returned to the reader 
to resume the reaction. Fluorescence signals were recorded at 1-minute intervals.

S1 Automated Hardware Platform 
S1.1 Hardware Architecture 
The platform is composed of five specialized workstations, each dedicated to a core function: 
nucleic acid synthesis, amplification, fluorescence detection, sequencing library preparation, and 
sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 2). Each workstation is equipped not only with the core 
instrumentation required for its specific task (e.g., a sequencing device, thermal cycler, or 
fluorometer) but also with detachable hardware modules (Supplementary Fig. 3). This modular 
design allows for functional expansion and reconfiguration through the assembly and replacement 
of these modules. The control modules inside the workstations are coordinated by software 
running atop an OpenHarmony operating system. 
For liquid handling, the system employs a two-tiered architecture. Intra-station transfers are 
performed by integrated automated pipetting systems and robotic arms, facilitating reagent 
transfer between any two instruments within a station. Furthermore, an AUBO-i10 robotic arm 
(Extended Data Fig. 1b) is responsible for inter-station transfers, thereby linking the workstations 
to enable more complex experimental workflows. For example, the workstation for synthesis can 
also be used for purification since it contains magnetic racks and thus can be used in a DNA 
storage experiment. The allocation of specific experiments supported in this work to these 
workstations is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Currently, the platform supports a total of 21 
distinct types of hardware, encompassing various models from different manufacturers.

S1.2 Auto-generation of AI-optimized Hardware Abstractions 
Existing instruments provide a series of Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) that 
applications can invoke to achieve automated operations. In AutoDNA, the agents automatically 
generate code by calling APIs and manage hardware resources based on them (Supplementary 
Notes 2.7). However, the native APIs provided by those instruments are often low-level; executing 
a single experimental operation may require the orchestration of multiple API calls, which poses a 
challenge for automated code generation and instrument management. 
To address this issue, we propose the concept of an AI-optimized abstract hardware layer. In 
AutoDNA, a specialized agent automatically generates a suitable hardware abstraction for 
subsequent use in code generation and instrument management. Supplementary Table S2 
illustrates this process using a pipette as an example. To simplify development, we instructed the 
agent to generate a hardware abstraction (as a Python class) containing a minimal set of methods 
and parameters, while also abstracting away the details of resource scheduling (Supplementary 
Notes 2.6). The prompt provided to the agent also included the hardware manual and pre-written 
support code. 
Consequently, the agent-generated Pipette class contains only two methods (mix and transfer), 
each with detailed usage documentation (docstrings) to facilitate subsequent automated code 
generation. In contrast, the manufacturer's manual lists 19 distinct API calls for the pipette, many 
of which are overly granular, coordinate-based movement operations. The agent successfully 
abstracted these into operations with higher-level experimental semantics. Once the AI-optimized 
hardware abstraction is obtained, we provide a translation module that maps the abstract calls to 
their concrete low-level implementations, thereby enabling the automated execution of the 
experiment. The hardware abstractions generated by agents are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

S1.3 Reproducibility and Transferability 
We mainly focus on two aspects of reproducibility and transferability: the stability of repeated 
experiments on the AutoDNA platform, and the ability to be transferred to other platforms. In 
experiments we tested, this method ensured high reproducibility. For instance, NAT demonstrated 
high consistency across three runs. Similarly, for DNA synthesis, we synthesized 78 DNA strands 
with distinct sequences, all of which exhibited consistent yields. These strands were synthesized in 
parallel, and sequencing results confirmed that neither throughput nor cross-contamination was 
compromised. 
As for the stability, the current AI-optimized hardware abstractions prioritize simplicity to 
facilitate the model's understanding of hardware abstraction and enhance the quality of code 
generation. Therefore, hardware-specific parameters (e.g., flow rates for pipettes) are hidden from 
models and configured with a default value.  
As for the ability to be transferred to other platforms, since the AI-optimized abstraction lacks the 
capability to configure parameters such as flow rates, air gaps, free or wet dispense, and tip-
touching, we assessed how they may affect the reproducibility in aspects below:  
(1) Precision and volumetric drift: We used standard DNA quantification experiments to 
evaluate the reproducibility of AutoDNA by varying four hardware-specific parameters, 
including various flow rates, air gaps, free or wet dispense, and with/without tip-touching. The 
quantification experiments were performed on AutoDNA's hardware platform using the Qubit
™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Q33231). The fluorometer was first calibrated following 
the manufacturer's operating instructions. The samples were prepared in 0.2 mL 8-strip tubes 
by combining (200 – x) µL of 1× dsDNA working buffer with x µL of DNA standard (2.5 
ng/µL) to achieve a final volume of 200 µL (where x = 2, 5, 10, or 20). After gentle pipette 
mixing and a 2-minute incubation at room temperature, the samples were transferred to the 
fluorometer for measurement. The experiment was performed independently three times for 
each parameter configuration and target volume. Across all configurations, the measured 
fluorescence intensity exhibited a strong linear correlation with dsDNA standard volume, 
yielding coefficients of determination (R2) exceeding 0.995 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The 
results confirm that the AutoDNA's automated pipetting machine maintains high linearity and 
pipetting accuracy within the 2–20 µL range, demonstrating its precision. Moreover, for each 
type of hardware parameter, the relative range of the mean fluorescence intensity across 
different parameter settings at the same dispensing volume remained below 5.0% (0.3%–
4.8%), indicating low operational variability and volumetric drift despite changes in hardware-
specific settings. These results demonstrate that AutoDNA's automated pipette machines retain 
high reproducibility even when hardware-specific parameters are varied.

= 3 independent experiments. 
 
(2) Cross-contamination: Since the pipette tips used by AutoDNA contain an internal filter that

S1.4 Human-involved Hardware Maintenance 
Human intervention in AutoDNA's workflow was confined to hardware maintenance and material-
management tasks, mainly including pre-experiment preparation of reagents and labware, on-
demand replenishment during experiments, and post-experiment waste disposal and platform 
cleanup. These manual operations did not participate in experimental design, result analysis, or 
autonomous decision-making by agents, and therefore did not affect the autonomous analysis and 
decision-making processes of AutoDNA agents. 
Before an experiment: to use AutoDNA for different types of experiments, the operator selects a 
preconfigured reagent-library configuration for the Reagent Agent according to the experiment to 
be executed. The operator then prepares the required reagent volumes and sufficient labware, 
aliquots reagents into designated containers, and places reagent containers, pipette tips, well plates 
and other labware at the corresponding positions on the hardware platform. 
During an experiment: manual intervention mainly involves on-demand replenishment of 
reagents and labware. Each workstation is equipped with a designated storage area for new 
labware and reagents, including pipette tips, well plates, tube strips and reagent reservoirs. During 
execution, the scheduler continuously monitors the remaining quantities of labware and the 
remaining volumes of reagents on the platform. When the system determines that a reagent or 
labware is insufficient for subsequent steps, it pauses the ongoing workflow and notifies the 
operator through the user interface to replenish the corresponding materials (Supplementary Notes 
2.7.2.3). The operator may also remove accumulated waste during replenishment. The number of 
manual replenishment events required during wet-lab execution for different experiments is 
summarized below (Supplementary Table S6).

After an experiment: the operator cleans the hardware platform. A dedicated waste-collection area 
is established on the AutoDNA platform. During experiments, used labware, such as spent pipette 
tips, reaction tubes and well plates, are automatically discarded into this area by the robotic arm or 
pipetting machines. Waste liquid is also discharged into designated waste-liquid areas. After the 
experiment is completed, the operator removes discarded labware and waste liquid from the waste-

collection areas and clears residual reagent containers, tube strips, well plates and other disposable 
labware remaining on the platform. To ensure experimental accuracy and prevent cross-
contamination, labware used by AutoDNA, including pipette tips, reaction tubes and well plates, 
are single-use only and are not cleaned or reused. 
AutoDNA also supports consecutive execution of different experiment types on the same platform. 
When switching between experiments, the operator does not need to perform additional manual 
adjustment of the hardware platform or complex software reconfiguration. The operator only needs 
to complete the cleanup after the previous experiment, prepare reagents and labware according to 
the requirements of the next experiment, and switch to the corresponding reagent-library 
configuration through the user interface. After these preparations, subsequent experimental 
execution, scheduling, replenishment and cleanup follow the same process as a single experiment 
run. 
 
 
 
  
 
 
S2 Agents 
We built all agents atop Gemini 2.5 Pro model due to its superior generative performance 
compared to contemporaneous models.  
S2.1 Planner Agent 
The Planner Agent functions as the central orchestrator for the entire experimental design process. 
Operating on a ReAct framework in LangGraph, the agent interprets the user's high-level 
experimental objectives and directs the workflow by invoking the appropriate agents in a logical 
sequence. To make this delegation, the Planner Agent assesses the complete operational context—
including the initial request and all historical inputs and outputs—and uses the descriptions of the 
available agents to select the one best suited for the immediate task (Supplementary Fig. 6). After 
the agent finishes the execution, the output is then added to the history for future reference.  
Complex experimental tasks may require the integration of multiple, distinct experimental sub-
tasks for their completion. To manage such tasks, the Planner Agent performs an initial assessment 
before execution, summarizing the core scientific goal and estimating the overall complexity of 
the request. For tasks that are identified as complex, the Planner Agent decomposes the high-level 
objective into a series of structured experimental stages. These stages are then executed 
sequentially, with the completion of one stage initiating the next. Conversely, for simple, single-

stage tasks, the system bypasses this decomposition step and proceeds directly with the execution 
of the original experimental protocol.

S2.2 Literature Agent 
S2.2.1 Overview 
A Literature Agent was developed to retrieve specific information from a local repository of 
scientific literature. The agent processes a list of queries about an experiment and returns a 
detailed, compiled answer. 
To achieve high-accuracy retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), the agent utilizes PaperQA2, an 
optimized language model agent specializing in answering scientific questions from a collection of 
customized theses and documents. The embedding model used is text-embedding-004, and the 
large language model invoked within the PaperQA2 workflow is gemini-2.5-flash. 
Several customizations were implemented to adapt PaperQA2 to our specific requirements. The 
default indexing behavior was modified. For documents containing a Digital Object Identifier 
(DOI), we implemented UUID indexing. This prevents conflicts that arise when multiple 
documents, such as a paper and its supplementary information, share the same DOI. 
Furthermore, the context selection process for the final answer generation was altered. Instead of 
using summaries of the most relevant texts, the original text passages corresponding to these 
summaries are used as the input. This change ensures that critical details, such as the concentration 
of each reagent in a reaction system, are retained. 
To expand the retrieval scope, the configuration parameters were adjusted. The answer.evidence_k 
was set to 50, and agent.search_count was set to 16. To increase the level of details in the 
generated answers, answer.answer_max_sources was set to 30. parsing.chunk_size was set to 
8,000 to enhance context integrity with parsing.overlap set to 700 accordingly. The 
answer.answer_length prompt was configured to "YOUR ANSWER MUST BE STRICTLY 
IDENTICAL AS WHAT IS IN THE PAPER, WITH ACCURATE DETAILS. " to direct the 
language model to provide comprehensive responses. 
The local literature repository was accomplished by performing searches on Google Scholar using 
the keywords "DNA amplification," "DNA transcription," "DNA sequencing," and "DNA 
synthesis." via SerpAPI (https://serpapi.com/). The top 100 papers (excluding books) for each 
keyword were downloaded and added to the repository, resulting in a total of 400 papers. Access 
to three of the selected articles—"Comparative genomic hybridization: a rapid new method for 

detecting and mapping DNA amplification in tumors", "Rapid cycle DNA amplification: time and 
temperature optimization", and "From deoxynucleotides to DNA synthesis"—was prevented by 
copyright restrictions. Consequently, they were substituted with the subsequent papers in the 
search rankings. Additionally, 11 essential documents, including manuals for reagents and 
necessary supplementary information, were incorporated into the repository.

Searches scientific literature and documents to answer specific questions and collects information 
about an experiment.  
 
    This agent performs targeted information retrieval from a local database of scientific papers

    Args:

S2.2.2 Ablation study 
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our modifications to the PaperQA module, we conducted a 
comparative experiment using the literature retrieval scenario from the enzymatic de novo 
synthesis workflow. In this test, the Planner Agent posed two specific questions: "What is the 
state-of-the-art stepwise yield for enzymatic de novo DNA synthesis?", and "What is the general 
procedure for enzymatic de novo DNA synthesis of ACTCTGAT?" 
Supplementary Tables S7 and S8 present the responses generated by the PaperQA module before 
and after our modifications, respectively. A clear improvement is evident: the revised version 
incorporates a broader range of sources, provides more granular detail, and demonstrates a more 
accurate comprehension of the input query. Specifically, the paper by Lu et al. (2022) describes 
both a TdT functional validation (single-base addition) and a multi-base synthesis protocol. The 
original PaperQA module erroneously conflated these two procedures, incorrectly applying 
reaction conditions (e.g., TdT concentration, reaction termination method, incubation time) from 
the validation experiment to the multi-base synthesis task. Furthermore, because the module 
performed an intermediate summarization of the source material before generating the final 

answer, a critical detail—the formulation of the deblocking buffer—was omitted. 
In contrast, the modified version, which integrates information from multiple sources directly into 
its final response, was able to answer the query with higher accuracy (e.g., using the correct TdT 
concentration and termination method) and provide additional details, such as the wash buffer 
formulation. Crucially, as the revised module bypasses the intermediate summarization step, it 
successfully retrieved and included the deblocking buffer formulation.

S2.3 Protocol Agent 
The Protocol Agent is the component for architecting the experimental plan. Its core responsibility 
is to generate, refine, and validate a detailed, step-by-step experimental procedure from various 
sources of information. 

The agent operates in four distinct modes:  
- Mode1 (initialization): create a new procedure from foundational information about an 
experiment.  
- Mode2 (refinement): refine an existing procedure in a two-stage process: the agent first 
incorporates new information, such as from technical manuals, to add detail and procedural 
options, and then filters all the options based on reagent availability.  
- Mode3 (adjustment): filter the input procedure against a list of available materials to remove 
infeasible options. 
- Mode4 (optimization): modify an existing procedure by applying specific optimization 
suggestions to alter its operational details, reagent choice, or steps.  
The agent has a mode-choosing controller to determine which operational mode to execute. This 
decision depends on the type of the input files: the controller analyzes the metadata of the current 
and historical inputs—identifying, for example, whether they are information from the Literature 
Agent, reagent validation results from the Reagent Agent, or are new supplementary manuals. By 
evaluating this information against a hierarchical set of rules, it selects the appropriate mode to 
address the specific requirements of the task at hand.  
In all modes, the agent's output adheres to a structured "part-step-option" format to ensure clarity 
and consistency. All but mode3 have a reagent checklist being appended after the main procedure 
to facilitate validation. 
A key characteristic of the agent is its ability to produce a highly structured, hierarchical output 
that details each part, step, and procedural option (Supplementary Fig. 7). The procedure is 
divided into major parts, each representing a distinct phase of the experiment. Within each part, 
discrete steps detail the specific actions to be performed. To accommodate experimental 
variations, each step may contain multiple options. Each option defines a distinct set of chemical 
components, which may be accompanied by corresponding operational details. This format 
consolidates multiple possible protocols into one to ensure that the information exchanged 
between agents is more compact, thereby reducing the likelihood of hallucinations.  

 
Supplementary Fig. 7 | An example of hierarchical protocols generated by the Protocol Agent. 
 
Complex experimental tasks may contain sub-tasks only requiring execution in silico (e.g., the 
encoding subtask in the DNA storage application). For those subtasks, the protocol agent 
constructs a complete algorithmic workflow based on the documents and the current experimental 
objective, which can be executed without requiring access to hardware experimental equipment.

The agent's reagent availability validation process begins when it receives an input file containing 
a reagent checklist, which includes the list of required reactants and buffers. The agent then 
validates the availability of each reagent via a database lookup operation. Once the verification 

process is complete, the agent produces a report that confirms the status of each item on the 
checklist, provides specifications for available materials, and flags those that are unavailable. It 
follows a strict output format like the output of the procedure agent, aiming for clarity and 
readability for other agents, reducing parsing errors.  
The reagent database lookup operation is achieved with a LLM-powered fuzzy matching, which is 
necessary because a single reagent can have multiple names and thus cannot be captured by an 
exact matching method (e.g., regular expressions). To enhance the correctness of LLM-powered 
matching, we add several predefined rules to its prompt. The first matching rule is that the 
existence of a buffer does not imply that its individual components are available, which provides 
clarity when matching a single substance. The second rule is that for buffers and solutions, if their 
components are specified, the availability is determined by a principle of exact composition, 
where an identical match of the specified chemical recipe is required. Otherwise, it's matched with 
their names.

S2.5 Hypothesis Agent 
The hypothesis agent is the core reasoning component responsible for the automated optimization 
of experimental protocols. Its primary inputs are the specific metrics targeted for improvement 
(i.e., yield or time), the protocol that produced the current result, and the outcome of the last 
experiment. To ensure continuous and informed improvement, the agent also maintains and 
consults a comprehensive history of past optimization attempts, including the proposal given 
before and the resulting experimental outcomes. This historical context is crucial, as it prevents the 
agent from repeating failed hypotheses and guides it toward more promising avenues of 

exploration. 
The agent's workflow is centered on a structured, multi-step reasoning process. First, it enters a 
hypothesis generation phase, where it brainstorms a diverse list of potential root causes—spanning 
physical, chemical, and biological factors—that could explain the suboptimal result. Crucially, it 
filters this brainstorming against its historical data to avoid previously invalidated hypotheses. 
From this list, the agent performs a selection step to identify the single most probable hypothesis 
to investigate. The agent then distills this hypothesis into a key phrase defining the root cause of 
the suboptimal outcome. Based on this analysis, it formulates a corrective action by cross-
referencing the experimental protocol with its internal knowledge and the provided instrument 
documentation. Some of the typical suggestions include modifying a reagent concentration or 
adjusting an incubation time. 
A key capability of the agent is its ability to learn from failure through a rollback mechanism. If an 
experiment confirms that an optimization advice was ineffective, the agent can be instructed to 
discard that attempt. This action marks the corresponding hypothesis in its history as failed and 
triggers a new cycle of hypothesis generation, forcing the agent to explore alternative 
explanations.  
Agent Description (viewed by the planner agent):  
Generates an optimization advice for an experimental procedure based on its history and a 
specified optimization target. 
    Args: 
        current_optimize_target (str): The target to be optimized. Only supports a single target. 
        roll_back_num (int): The number of recent, ineffective optimization attempts to discard. 
        current_procedure_id (str): The ID of the experimental procedure that is being optimized. 
        manuals (list[str], optional): An optional list of file IDs for manuals that were collected 
during the process, which may inform the optimization advice. 
    Returns: 
        str: A formatted string containing the new optimization advice and a reference to its 
saved file ID. 
 
 
S2.6 Code Agent 
S2.6.1 Overview 
The Code Agent is a component designed to translate an experimental procedure into executable 
Python scripts that operate laboratory instruments. This agent receives the validated experimental 
procedure as its primary input, but it can also refer to the AI-optimized hardware abstractions 
(Supplementary Notes 1.2) to generate hardware-compliant code.  

To ensure the generated code is contextually aware and correct, it is also provided with hardware 
abstractions. The abstraction layer creates a crucial separation between the experimental logic and 
the underlying hardware, containing a simplified set of high-level functions. By generating code 
that calls these methods, the agent can construct valid experimental scripts without managing low-
level hardware details. This approach significantly enhances the modularity and portability of the 
entire system, as the high-level code remains unchanged even if physical instruments are 
reconfigured.  
Since the input may contain a tree-based structure to maintain multiple protocols, the Code Agent 
is responsible for decomposing it into distinct, linear paths. For each path representing a protocol, 
the Code Agent generates a complete and independent script. Furthermore, the agent incorporates 
a self-correction step: it simulates the execution of generated scripts in a sandboxed environment 
to verify their correctness before they are used in a live experiment. The sandbox assumes the 
hardware will run correctly, so the primary errors checked are syntactic issues using the hardware 
API and basic state management errors. If an error is detected, the Code Agent uses the error 
message to regenerate the code in a self-correction loop until the simulated execution is 
successful.  
Finally, the Code Agent outputs a set of correct scripts with each starting with "### SCRIPT 
START ###" and a simple description of the options chosen in the script. 
For subtasks only requiring execution in silico, the Code Agent generates software-only code 
without using the AI-optimized hardware abstractions. Instead, the usage guidelines of command-
line toolkits (e.g., bioinformatics software such as samtools) are provided to the Code Agent. This 
approach clearly determines the tools available for code generation and thus enhance the usability 
of the code produced by the agent.  
 
 
Agent Description (viewed by the planner agent): 
    Generates the Python code script for a validated experiment procedure. 
    Args: 
       code_request_id (str): A file_id containing the experiment procedure to be transformed to 
code. 
        pure_software (bool): A flag indicating if the task is purely software-based. 
        multiple_inputs (str): If there are multiple different inputs for the protocol, then fill the 
parameter with all of the different inputs. Otherwise, leave it empty. 

        inputs_from_previous_stage (str): If the outputs from the previous stage have something

information from the previous stage that may serves as inputs. 
    Returns:

S2.6.2 Hardware-independent Code Generation 
The movement of reagents and their associated vessels is a critical step in the experimental 
process. To facilitate the specification of precise locations, existing automated laboratory 
equipment typically abstracts the internal positions of the instrument. For instance, the Opentrons 
pipetting workstation2, commonly used in previous work, provides an abstraction based on 
labware slots and well positions. Applications can use these indices to designate the original 
location of reagents and their target destinations, thereby simplifying operations for the pipetting 
module (Supplementary Fig. 9). 
 
Supplementary Fig. 9 | Location-based abstraction and a related pipetting code example. 

 
Due to the requirement for supporting multi-protocol/multi-user experiments, AutoDNA 
necessitates dynamic scheduling of multiple experimental workflows (Supplementary Notes 6). To 
address this, the multi-agent architecture of AutoDNA decouples code generation (Code Agent) 
from physical hardware execution (Hardware Agent). Since the Code Agent does not perceive 
hardware-specific information, relying on location abstraction for code generation could result in 
multiple code segments mapping to the same well slot, leading to conflicts during concurrent 
execution and reduced experimental efficiency. 
To resolve this issue, AutoDNA introduces a location-agnostic container abstraction. It abstracts 
various reagent-bearing vessels (such as tubes of different models, 96-well plates, etc.) into virtual 
containers. Each container represents a liquid-holding space that can be flexibly bound to any 
suitable physical location as required. 
The container abstraction is organized in the form of Python objects (Supplementary Table S9) for 
generating AI-optimized hardware abstractions and experimental control code. Each container 
primarily includes four attributes: index (a unique identifier), volume (current liquid volume), 
capacity (maximum volume), and location (current physical position). In addition to the container 
abstraction, AutoDNA provides a ContainerManager class—an abstraction for container 
management—which includes a method called newContainer for allocating new containers. Both 
classes, together with other AI-optimized abstractions, are provided to the Code Agent for code 
generation.

S2.6.3 Simulation-based Self-correction  
To enable self-correction in the Code Agent, AutoDNA designed a simulator atop the AI-
optimized hardware abstraction layer. This simulator translates each invocation to the hardware 
abstraction interface into internal state transitions of the corresponding instruments. It assumes all 
hardware operations can be completed successfully and primarily focuses on the following three 
types of issues: 
l Liquid volume: When a pipetting operation occurs, the simulator updates the volume attribute 
of the relevant container accordingly. By maintaining liquid volumes, it can detect issues such 
as insufficient volume or exceeding a container’s capacity. 
l Labware location: When a robotic arm movement is simulated, the simulator updates the 

location attribute of the involved containers. This helps identify problems, including idle 
instrument operations or positional conflicts. 
l Instrument properties: Instruments often include configurable attributes—such as the shaking 
frequency of a heater-shaker—that can be set via interfaces in the hardware abstraction. The 
simulator models these properties and updates them when the corresponding interface is 
called, enabling detection of invalid configurations (e.g., setting a frequency beyond the 
maximum allowable value). 
When an error is detected, the simulator throws an exception to the Code Agent, accompanied by a 
detailed error description. Upon receiving the exception, the Code Agent regenerates an updated 
version of the code based on the current code context and error explanation. The new code then 
undergoes the same simulation and self-correction cycle iteratively until it passes all checks. Only 
then is the validated code returned to the Planner Agent to proceed to the next step.

Code Agent simulated its execution and detected that no container was placed on the fluorometer 
when calling fluorometer.measure(). The simulator then raised an exception and provided a repair 
suggestion, based on which the Code Agent added a moveContainer operation to enable proper 
fluorescence detection.

S2.7 Hardware Agent 
S2.7.1 Overview 
The Hardware Agent is the component that bridges the digital experimental plan with the physical 
laboratory environment. Its primary responsibility is to manage the deployment and execution of 
the final Python scripts on the automated hardware platform via a sophisticated runtime scheduler. 
This agent oversees the entire execution process. It dispatches the code to the scheduler, which in 
turn converts abstract commands into specific instructions for the instruments.  
Upon completion of the experiment, the Hardware Agent generates a notification in its user 
interface to inform the user that the experiment has concluded and indicates the location of the 
experimental products. Simultaneously, according to user-defined requirements, the Hardware 
Agent autonomously infers the relevant experimental metrics to be reported. If obtaining these 
metrics depends on manual operations, the Agent provides a set of input fields in the user 
interface, prompting the user to enter the specific values of the required metrics. Once the user-
provided data is acquired, the Hardware Agent associates the results with the corresponding 
experimental protocol ID and returns them to the Planner Agent for subsequent optimization and 
analysis. If multiple code scripts were executed in parallel, the Hardware Agent identifies the one 
that produced the optimal result and only reports the result along with its corresponding protocol 
ID.  
The Hardware Agent has a virtual computation device to support subtasks only executed in silico. 
For this case, the agent provides an execution environment (i.e., Python) for the program and 
returns the results once the program finishes. The scheduler is not used in this execution mode.  

 
Agent Description (viewed by the planner agent):  
Executes experimental Python code and retrieves the results.

S2.7.2 Runtime Scheduler 
The Runtime Scheduler (hereafter, “the scheduler”) is the software system responsible for the 
physical execution of experiments, invoked by the Hardware Agent. Built upon the AI-optimized 
hardware abstraction layer, the scheduler translates high-level API calls from the generated code 
into low-level hardware control commands. A key function of the scheduler is to enhance 
experimental efficiency in concurrent multi-protocol and multi-user scenarios. It achieves this 
through two primary strategies: static code merging and dynamic resource scheduling. 
S2.7.2.1 Static Code Merging 
When the scheduler receives multiple experimental requests, it identifies and merges compatible 
operations targeting the same instrument to leverage its parallel processing capabilities. The 
process is as follows: 
First, the scheduler abstracts the code generated by the Code Agent into a sequence of atomic 
service invocations (Supplementary Fig. 11). It then employs sequence alignment algorithms3 to 
identify and associate similar subsequences between two distinct protocols. Two atomic service 
invocations are deemed similar if they meet the following criteria: 
1. They must be invocations of the same atomic service. 
2. All their parameters, excluding the container identifiers, must be identical. 
3. The merged operation must not exceed the pre-defined throughput constraints of the target 
hardware (provided manually to the Scheduler). 
The merging process is performed iteratively, pairing two sequences at a time until no further 
merges are possible. Upon completion of the merging phase, the scheduler utilizes the simulator 
(as described previously) to perform a validation run, ensuring the correctness of the merged code. 

An example of this process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 11, where three distinct protocols 
for four devices with varying throughput limits are consolidated into a single, unified protocol for 
execution, thereby improving efficiency. During the subsequent dynamic resource scheduling 
phase, containers from merged operations are mapped to physically adjacent positions (e.g., within 
the same 8-tube strip) to facilitate simultaneous manipulation by high-throughput hardware. 
 
Supplementary Fig. 11 | Schematic diagram of static code merging. 
 
However, the aforementioned merging algorithm can be overly aggressive in certain scenarios, 
leading to execution errors. Supplementary Fig. 12 provides an example: three distinct pipetting 
operations from three protocols are merged. Consequently, their respective containers (1, 2, and 3) 
are co-located in adjacent slots of an 8-tube strip to enable simultaneous pipetting. The issue arises 
because the robotic arm, which also operates at the level of an 8-tube strip, is only instructed to 
move the strip in protocols 1 and 2. Nevertheless, the entire strip—including container 3—is 
transported to a heater for incubation. This results in an erroneous workflow for the third protocol. 
To mitigate such issues arising from physical hardware constraints, the scheduler uses the 
simulator for validation.  If an error occurs during simulated execution, the scheduler marks steps 
that cannot be merged and re-attempts the merging process from the beginning. This iterative 
process continues until no further operations can be merged. If the simulation encounters an error, 
the merge operation is discarded, and the original, unmerged code sequences are executed instead. 

 
Supplementary Fig. 12 | An example of incorrect code merging. 
 
S2.7.2.2 Dynamic Resource Scheduling 
The primary role of the dynamic resource scheduling module is to map the virtual containers 
referenced in atomic service invocations to specific physical locations and to execute the 
underlying hardware control code to complete each operation. To this end, the module 
continuously maintains a real-time availability status for all physical instruments and uses this 
information to guide the mapping process. 
A key feature of this module is its handling of resource conflicts. If a target instrument for a given 
container is unavailable (e.g., currently in use), the scheduler pauses the execution and notifies the 
Hardware Agent. The Hardware Agent then requests the Code Agent to identify and select an 
alternative instrument with functionally equivalent capabilities (Fig. 4b). Once a substitute is 
designated, the scheduler resumes execution with the new resource assignment. if the new 
instrument requires a different type of labware (e.g., a switch from individual tubes to a 96-well 

plate), the scheduler will also dynamically generate the necessary liquid handling steps to transfer 
the samples. This dynamic re-routing mechanism ensures maximal utilization of the platform's 
hardware resources by redirecting tasks to available instruments on the fly. 
 
S2.7.2.3 Replenishment 
In addition to the aforementioned functionalities, the scheduler also provides reminders when 
reagents and labware (such as pipette tips, tubes and well plates) are running low. The scheduler is 
responsible for monitoring the remaining liquid volume and labware quantities. First, the 
scheduler needs to track the initial input reagents and labware to monitor. For reagents, we 
implemented a getContainerForReplenish method within the containerManager abstraction. This 
method allows the Code Agent to retrieve and mark the container holding the initial input reagents 
for an experiment. For labware, the scheduler assumes those located in the designated storage 
areas on each workstation as new and tracks their usage. 
When the scheduler detects insufficient liquid volume or labware quantities, it pauses the 
execution of the corresponding experimental workflow and triggers a user interface notification 
prompting reagent/labware replenishment (Supplementary Fig. 13). For reagent replenishment, the 
notification includes details such as the type of reagent to be replenished, its location in the 
labware layout, and the required volume. For labware replenishment, the notification specifies the 
type and quantity of labware needed, as well as their precise placement location on the 
workstation. The experimental workflow resumes only after the operator has restocked the 
reagents/labware and confirmed completion via the interactive interface. 
 
 

 
Supplementary Fig. 13 | The user interface for reagent and labware replenishment. a, Interface 
for reagent replenishment. b, Interface for labware replenishment. 
S2.7.2.4 Uninterruptible Experiments 
Biological experiments would mandate strict temporal continuity, where any unintended 
interruption may lead to experimental failure. For such protocols, users can designate them as 
"uninterruptible" in the experimental prompt. This constraint is interpreted by the Planner Agent 
and relayed to the scheduler within the Hardware Agent. The scheduler then identifies the required 
instruments and initiates the experiment only when simultaneous availability of all required 
instruments is confirmed. Upon initiation, the scheduler exclusively reserves these instruments 
until the experiment concludes, preventing allocation to other tasks. By eliminating resource 
contention during execution, this approach ensures zero‑delay transitions between experimental 
steps and mitigates reproducibility issues caused by unintended pauses. 
 
S2.8 Agent Extensions 
The multi-agent architecture of AutoDNA is designed for straightforward extensibility. Integrating 
a new agent into the system involves the definition of two primary components: an agent 
description and a prompt. The prompt specifies the agent’s operational workflow. This typically 
includes defining its expected inputs, the sequence of its internal execution steps, and the format 
of its output. The description provides a high-level, functional summary of the agent’s capabilities. 
This description is registered with and utilized by the central Planner Agent. Once equipped with 
this information, the Planner Agent incorporates the new agent into its decision-making process 

for each subsequent execution step, evaluating it as a potential candidate for invocation. 
 
S3 Nucleic Acid Test 
S3.1 Introduction to RPA-based Nucleic Acid Test 
Nucleic acid testing (NAT) determines the presence of target microorganisms in a sample by 
amplifying and identifying specific pathogen gene sequences. Common methodologies include 
real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) and isothermal amplification techniques, such as Recombinase 
Polymerase Amplification (RPA), Loop-Mediated Isothermal Amplification (LAMP), and 
CRISPR-Cas-based systems. RPA is a highly sensitive isothermal nucleic acid amplification 
technique. It operates at a constant low temperature (39–42 °C) and eliminates the need for 
thermal cycling. The amplification process is initiated by a pair of primers and mediated 
synergistically by a recombinase-primer complex, single-stranded DNA-binding protein (SSB), 
and a strand-displacing DNA polymerase. RPA exhibits versatility, with applications spanning 
from pathogen detection to next-generation sequencing library preparation, and is particularly 
well-suited for rapid screening of diverse pathogens in field settings. For instance, RPA has been 
successfully applied to the rapid detection of African swine fever virus (ASFV). ASFV is a 
significant pathogen responsible for a lethal disease in swine. It can spread rapidly via cross-
border logistics, causing severe impacts on the swine industry and raising food safety concerns. 
Consequently, efficient on-site detection methods are urgently required to block its transmission.  
In this work, AutoDNA leverages an existing RPA-based nucleic acid detection assay (Lesunbio 
DNA Thermostatic Nucleic Acid Amplification Kit (Basic Version), Product Code DNA-LS01), 
targeting the highly conserved p72-encoding gene (B646L) fragment of ASFV. Agents in 
AutoDNA performed primer binding, strand displacement, and extension reactions at a constant 
temperature of 39 °C, enabling rapid amplification of the target nucleic acid without reliance on 
PCR thermal cycling.

S3.3 Test results 
To evaluate the detection efficacy of AutoDNA, we conducted parallel comparative experiments 
against manual operation. AutoDNA achieves full-process automation, encompassing reagent 
preparation, sample addition, mixing, and automated transfer of the reaction mixture to a thermal 
cycler for isothermal amplification. Furthermore, AutoDNA acquired fluorescence signals at 5-
minute intervals during the amplification process, recording data at a total of 6 time points 
(including the initial value). Results from three independent parallel experiments demonstrated 
that the detection performance of AutoDNA was comparable to manual operation (Fig. 2 and 
Extended Data Fig. 4), with no significant difference observed. At a positive template 
concentration as low as 2.5 copies/μL, both methods detected a distinct fluorescence signal 
(threshold set at 3 times the fluorescence signal of the no-template control, NTC). The negative 
control group containing blank plasmid (1 ng/μL pU57) showed no significant difference from the 
NTC. When the template concentration was ≥25 copies/μL, a significant increase in fluorescence 
signal (≥5,000 a.u.; negative control, NC, and NTC fluorescence signals were approximately 
1,000 a.u.) was detectable within 10 minutes, enabling rapid ASFV detection.  
In conclusion, with detection sensitivity comparable to manual operation, AutoDNA enables batch 
nucleic acid testing under unattended conditions. This effectively avoids the potential risk of 
operators being exposed to pathogens, not only saving time costs for nucleic acid testing but also 
eliminating labor costs. 
 
S4 siRNA 
S4.1 Introduction to siRNA synthesis 
Ribonucleic acid (RNA) is a pivotal macromolecule central to the transmission and expression of 
genetic information. Its primary biological role is to direct protein synthesis, but it also plays 
critical roles in genetic coding, translation, and the regulation of gene expression. Functionally, 

RNA molecules are classified into several major types, including messenger RNA (mRNA), 
transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and small interfering RNA (siRNA). 
Advances in molecular biology have enabled the in vitro synthesis of RNA molecules with user-
defined sequences, a capability that has been foundational for studies of gene function and 
regulation. The primary methodologies for artificial RNA synthesis include in vitro transcription 
(IVT) from linear DNA templates, solid-phase chemical synthesis, and enzymatic synthesis4-7. The 
continued refinement of these techniques has driven the application of synthetic RNA in both 
basic research and medicine, most notably in the development of oligonucleotide therapeutics. 
Oligonucleotide therapeutics represent a distinct class of drugs compared to traditional small-
molecule compounds and antibody-based biologics. These functional nucleic acids operate by 
selectively targeting a specific gene or its corresponding mRNA to modulate gene expression and 
regulate translation-related processes, thereby achieving a targeted therapeutic effect8. This class 
includes antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and microRNAs 
(miRNAs). The mechanism of RNA interference, mediated by siRNAs, is particularly noteworthy, 
having been recognized with the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine9. 
siRNAs are double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) molecules, typically 21-23 nucleotides in length, that 
can efficiently silence or knock down the expression of a target gene. This function has led to their 
extensive use in both fundamental genetic research and therapeutic development. For the past two 
decades, scientists have continued to explore the application of siRNAs for treating cancer, viral 
infections, and genetic disorders10,11. This therapeutic potential was realized in 2018 with the FDA 
approval of Patisiran, the first siRNA-based drug, for the treatment of hereditary transthyretin-
mediated amyloidosis12. Consequently, numerous pharmaceutical companies, including Alnylam, 
Ionis, and Novartis, are now heavily investing in and developing pipelines for oligonucleotide 
therapeutics. This growing clinical and commercial interest has, in turn, fueled an increasing 
demand for high-quality, large-scale synthetic RNA. 
We demonstrate the end-to-end autonomous service capabilities of AutoDNA with the synthesis of 
two siRNAs. The synthesis was performed via in vitro transcription and highlighted the platform's 
potential for the scalable manufacturing of small nucleic acid therapeutics.

S4.3.3 Fluorescence Results 
Extended Data Fig. 5 displays the results of a strand displacement fluorescence assay conducted to 
validate the correctness of the RNA sequence produced by AutoDNA. The fluorescence intensity 
data shown herein have been normalized. The experiment included three groups: (a) positive 
control, (b) RNA generated by AutoDNA (experimental group), and (c) negative control. 
The results indicate that the positive control group (a) exhibited a rapid increase in fluorescence 
intensity over time, eventually reaching a plateau, confirming the effectiveness of the detection 
assay. This demonstrates that the correct RNA sequence can efficiently displace the quencher and 
liberate the fluorophore via strand displacement. The experimental sample group (b)—RNA 
synthesized by AutoDNA—also showed a pronounced rise in fluorescence signal, with a kinetic 
curve similar to that of the positive control and well above the background level. This indicates 
that AutoDNA successfully produced recognizable RNA sequences. In contrast, no notable 
fluorescence signal was observed in the negative control group (c), which contained incorrect 
sequences, or in the no-template control (NTC) across all groups, confirming the high specificity 
of the assay. 
It should be noted that the RNA sequence (RSseq) used in this experiment is a random sequence 
and does not represent an siRNA14 targeting any specific gene. This sequence was chosen because 
certain target siRNA sequences (e.g., SARSi-1) are unsuitable for this validation system due to 
structural constraints. Thus, the RSseq sequence serves as a model to specifically evaluate 
AutoDNA’s capability in RNA transcription and purification.

S5.1 Agent workflow 
Upon receiving a request for enzymatic de novo synthesis, the agent-based system converged on a 
workflow that satisfied the user-defined optimization objectives after 11 iterations (two of which 
are not shown in Fig. 3 as they are aborted), comprising a total of 95 steps (Supplementary Table 
S16). The optimization process proceeded as follows: 
In the first iteration, the Protocol Agent formulated a preliminary protocol based on literature, 
encompassing procedures for initial DNA (iDNA) immobilization and subsequent purification. 

Meanwhile, it also learned the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) yield (98.7% from Lu et al.). 
However, after the Reagent Agent was consulted to verify the status of the starting materials, it 
was determined that the iDNA (sequences in Supplementary Table S17) was already supplied pre-
immobilized on magnetic beads. This finding prompted the Protocol Agent to prune the now-
redundant immobilization step from the protocol.  
During this interaction with the Reagent Agent (Step 6), the Planner Agent also received the 
manufacturers' protocols for the magnetic beads and the terminator nucleotide. This information 
was subsequently relayed to the Protocol Agent. This finally led to the generation of two viable 
protocol variants, which differed primarily in the choice of wash buffer (Lysis buffer and B&W 
buffer). Since the required concentrations of the lysis and B&W buffers did not match those in the 
reagent inventory, the Code Agent generated the necessary code to perform the dilution (Step 14).  
To select the optimal path, the Hardware Agent was tasked with executing the code corresponding 
to both protocols. Based on the empirical results, the protocol utilizing the B&W buffer was 
determined to be superior. This optimized protocol was subsequently saved to a file and returned 
as the output of the first iteration. 
Since the reported experimental yield failed to meet the predefined objective, the Planner Agent 
initiated a self-optimization workflow and determined the yield as the current optimization target 
(Step 17). Therefore, it invoked the Hypothesis Agent to propose optimization strategies on yield. 
The first hypothesis generated was to perform an additional deprotection round, and the Protocol 
and Code Agents generated the corresponding refinement. However, as the Hardware Agent 
reported a decreased yield in the subsequent experimental run, the Planner Agent automatically 
reverted this modification. 
Next, the Hypothesis Agent proposed substituting the deblocking and washing buffers. These 
suggestions were discarded after the Reagent Agent provided feedback indicating that the required 
reagents were not available in the inventory. The Hypothesis Agent then continued to generate 
further suggestions, such as adding Tween-20 surfactant and increasing the CoCl2 concentration. 
Through these iterative adjustments, the system progressively improved the yield to meet the 
requirement defined in the prompt, and the Planner Agent switched the optimization target to the 
overall experiment time. (note: subsequent iterations are summarized to highlight the hypotheses 
generated and their corresponding experimental outcomes). In the final iteration, the Hypothesis 
Agent proposed reducing the number of wash cycles. However, results indicated that this 
optimization would cause the synthesis yield to drop below 1% of the SOTA, thereby meeting the 
termination criteria described in the prompt ("When the stepwise yield drops more than 1% of the 
SOTA stepwise yield, you must stop optimizing"). At this point, the Planner Agent generated a 
summary of the entire process and archived the final, optimized protocol as a file for future 
retrieval and use.

Compared with existing methods based on predefined protocols and optimization dimensions 
(Supplementary Fig. 15), AutoDNA's final protocol was iteratively designed by LLM-powered 
agents that integrate information retrieved from literature with experimental results. By operating 
independently of expert rules and fixed algorithms (e.g., Bayesian optimization), this demonstrates 
AutoDNA's capability for optimization exploration within open dimensions. 

 
Supplementary Fig. 15 | AutoDNA versus existing Bayesian Optimization-based self-
optimization methods. 
 
In addition, this optimization process suggests that the optimization dimensions explored by 
AutoDNA include not only chemical parameters directly related to the enzymatic DNA synthesis 
reaction, but also operational parameters that may be treated implicitly in manual experiments. A 
representative example is the magnetic separation time. In a manual reproduction experiment 
conducted within our team, a senior PhD student with multiple years of experimental experience 
obtained a lower stepwise yield than the automated AutoDNA execution, despite using the same 
nominal experimental conditions (88.1% vs. 90.3%, Supplementary Fig. 16). Further analysis 
showed that, during the manual operation, the endpoint of the magnetic separation step was 
determined mainly by visual inspection of whether the supernatant had become clear, rather than 
by strictly following the separation time specified in the AutoDNA protocol. Because this 
parameter is not directly related to the core chemical reaction step of enzymatic synthesis, it can 
easily be treated as an experience-based operational detail in manual protocol design or execution, 
rather than as an explicit controllable condition. By incorporating such operational parameters into 
the automated protocol and executing them consistently, AutoDNA reduces the risk of omitting 
potential optimization dimensions when experimental workflows are manually defined or 
reproduced.

S5.2.2 PAGE Figures 
To validate the final protocol generated by the self-optimization process, we synthesized an 8-mer 
oligonucleotide with the sequence 5′-ACTCTGAT-3′. A 10% aliquot of the reaction mixture was 
collected after each nucleotide addition cycle to monitor the stepwise progress of the synthesis. 
These samples were then analyzed by gel electrophoresis (Fig. 3c, the corresponding uncropped 
and unprocessed gel image is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1). The resulting gel image displays 
a series of bands corresponding to the initial DNA (iDNA) and the products after the addition of 1 
through 8 nucleotides. A distinct ladder-like pattern is observed, with the DNA bands exhibiting a 
progressive upward mobility shift as the number of incorporated bases increases from left to right. 
This result is characteristic of a successful primer extension reaction and provides clear evidence 
that Terminal deoxynucleotidyl Transferase (TdT) sequentially incorporated the specified 
deoxynucleotides onto the initiator DNA, thereby achieving de novo synthesis of the target 
sequence. Extended Data Fig. 6 further shows the gel image and corresponding yield for each 
iteration, providing a detailed schematic of the decision-making pathway that AutoDNA navigated 
to systematically optimize the enzymatic DNA synthesis protocol. 
  
 

S5.2.3 Discussion of beads aggregation 
To investigate the physical state of the magnetic beads during synthesis, we manually paused the 
autonomous AutoDNA process at various stages to collect samples for analysis by optical 
microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 17). These observations revealed that the degree of bead 
aggregation became progressively more pronounced as the synthesis reaction continued. This 
phenomenon offers a potential explanation for why deletion is the predominant error type 
observed during the synthesis process, as beads within large aggregates may become physically 
inaccessible to reagents.

S5.3 Comparison with a fine-tuned Bayesian optimization baseline 
To further demonstrate the efficiency of the LLM-led multi-objective optimization strategy in 
AutoDNA, we designed a Bayesian optimization (BO) baseline tuned with literature-derived 
experimental data and compared its optimization efficiency with AutoDNA in the same enzymatic 
DNA synthesis task. 
 
S5.3.1 Literature data collection, screening, and standardization 
Before screening the literature, we applied two restrictions to the synthesis systems reported in the 
candidate studies: 
1. Use of the same sodium nitrite (NaNO₂)-based chemical deprotection system as AutoDNA; 
2. Use of the same engineered ZaTdT as AutoDNA (ZaTdT-R335L-K337G, commercially 
available as E-ZaTdT). 
Using Lu et al.15, which was cited in the main text of AutoDNA, as the starting point for literature 
search, we traced all papers citing this work on Google Scholar (108 papers as of 25 May 2026). 
We then selected papers that satisfied the two restrictions above and reported either stepwise yield 
or total yield data. In total, we identified 4 papers and 45 usable data points: 

- 
Lu et al. "Enzymatic DNA synthesis by engineering terminal deoxynucleotidyl 
transferase." ACS Catalysis 12.5 (2022): 2988-2997.  
- 
Li et al. "DNA-DISK: Automated end-to-end data storage via enzymatic single-
nucleotide DNA synthesis and sequencing on digital microfluidics." Proceedings of the 
National Academy of Sciences 121.34 (2024): e2410164121. 
- 
Li et al. "Highly Ordered DNA Framework Interface Enables Efficient Enzymatic 
Oligonucleotide Synthesis." Advanced Science 12.44 (2025): e05868.  
- 
Li et al. "Scalable acoustic virtual stirrer for enhanced interfacial enzymatic nucleic acid 
reactions." Science Advances 11.10 (2025): eadt6955.  
After determining the included papers, we extracted three types of information from each. The 
first type was experimental outcomes, including stepwise yield or total yield. The second type was 
experimental conditions describing the enzymatic DNA synthesis workflow, including base 
sequence, number of synthesis cycles, extension and deprotection reaction conditions, reagent 
concentrations, buffer conditions, and surfactant information. The third type was contextual 
variables used to distinguish different experimental systems, such as substrate type. For 
parameters that were not reported for each individual data point but were required in the 
experiment, we filled the missing values using the most commonly reported values among the 
included papers. For data that reported only total yield, we converted it to stepwise yield based on 
the number of synthesis cycles. All filling and conversion operations are documented in 
Supplementary Data 1. 
S5.3.2 Search Space Construction 
The core workflow of enzymatic DNA synthesis consists of alternating single-base extension 
and deprotection steps. The optimization experiments in AutoDNA were also built around this 
basic cycle, while exploring dimensions such as buffers, surfactants, reaction time, and 
reagent concentrations during iterative optimization. Therefore, to construct a BO baseline 
comparable to AutoDNA in the enzymatic synthesis task, we used the “extension-wash-
deprotection-wash” cycle adopted in the enzymatic synthesis experiments as the workflow 
template (Supplementary Table S19). We first summarized the controllable experimental 
parameters in this workflow, then added sequence features and substrate type as contextual 
variables to distinguish different experimental settings in the literature data, and finally aligned 
these dimensions with the variables extracted from the literature. Most dimensions were directly 
observed in the literature data. For the few dimensions that were insufficiently covered in the 
literature but controllable in the AutoDNA workflow or necessary to retain in the model, we used 
the following treatment: 

- 
Base sequence: In the workflow template, the base sequence is a single input variable, 
whereas the literature data contain sequences with different lengths and nucleotide 
compositions. To enable the GP model to use this information, we encoded each sequence 
into four descriptors: sequence length, GC content, maximum run length of identical bases, 
and whether the sequence is a homopolymer. When recommending wet-lab experimental 
conditions with BO, the target sequence was fixed, and these sequence descriptors were not 
treated as free optimization variables. 
- 
Deprotection temperature: None of the included papers explicitly reported the deprotection 
temperature. Because the corresponding experimental steps did not describe additional heating 
or cooling, we uniformly filled this value as room temperature, encoded as 25 °C. Since this 
temperature can be controlled in the AutoDNA workflow, we retained this dimension in the 
BO search space. 
- 
Wash count: The literature data were recorded using a single wash-count variable within 
each synthesis cycle. To align this variable with the two wash steps in the AutoDNA 
workflow, namely post-extension washing and post-deprotection washing, we split it into two 
variables: number of washes after extension and number of washes after deprotection. The 
original wash count in literature data was mapped to both dimensions. 
- 
Buffer and surfactant types: Candidate categories were defined according to the options 
available in the AutoDNA experimental workflow. 
- 
Substrate: The literature data contained multiple substrate types, which were used to 
represent different experimental systems. Because AutoDNA used a magnetic-bead system, 
this dimension was retained in the model but fixed to bead-MB during BO recommendation.

S5.3.3 BO Model Selection and Tuning 
We implemented the BO baseline using BoTorch. BoTorch is a commonly used open-source 
framework for Bayesian optimization research and applications16, providing standard 
implementations of Gaussian process (GP) surrogate models, multi-objective acquisition 

functions, and mixed-variable optimization. It is therefore well suited for constructing the BO 
baseline. Because the variable space contained continuous, integer, and categorical variables, we 
used MixedSingleTaskGP as the surrogate model. For multi-objective optimization, we used q-
Noisy Expected Hypervolume Improvement (qNEHVI)17 as the acquisition function. qNEHVI is a 
commonly used acquisition function for noisy multi-objective Bayesian optimization. This method 
and closely related variants have also been applied in automated chemical reaction optimization 
and self-driving laboratory studies18,19. By maximizing the expected hypervolume improvement, 
this acquisition function balances yield and experimental time without requiring predefined 
objective weights. 
To tune the BO model, we encoded the experimental conditions extracted from the literature data 
as input vectors in the BO variable space described above, used the corresponding stepwise yields 
as observations, and fitted the kernel parameters and observation-noise hyperparameters of the GP 
surrogate model by maximizing the exact marginal log likelihood (MLL). Experimental time was 
instead calculated using a deterministic time model based on workflow parameters, including 
extension time, deprotection time, number of deprotection rounds, number of washes, and 
magnetic separation time. 
 
S5.3.4 Wet-lab results and discussion 
After tuning the BO model, we applied it to the enzymatic DNA synthesis experiment. 
Specifically, the model was used to generate recommended experimental conditions, which were 
then executed using the automated hardware platform of AutoDNA. The experimental results, 
including stepwise yield and per-cycle time, were fed back to the BO model to generate the next 
recommendation. This closed-loop process was repeated for 9 rounds, matching the number of 
wet-lab iterations reported for AutoDNA. 
The tuned BO method identified a best stepwise yield of only 92.6% within 9 iterations, 
corresponding to a total yield of 54.4% (Supplementary Fig. 18). This result was substantially 
lower than both the highest yield obtained during AutoDNA optimization (98.0% stepwise yield 
and 85.1% total yield) and the time-balanced condition selected by AutoDNA (97.8% stepwise 
yield and 83.5% total yield). In addition, the BO method showed limited exploration of time-

Building on this result, we further analyzed the expected hypervolume improvement (E[HVI]) of 
the qNEHVI acquisition function along the extension-time axis (Supplementary Fig. 19). 
Specifically, we used the fifth-round BO wet-lab condition as the baseline, varied the extension time 
from 2 to 20 min while holding all other experimental variables fixed, and calculated the 
corresponding per-cycle experimental time using the deterministic time model. For the model using 
only literature data and the first five rounds of BO wet-lab feedback, E[HVI] decreased as extension 
time and per-cycle time increased, indicating that the GP surrogate had not learned an effective 
signal that extending the extension time could substantially improve yield. Therefore, qNEHVI 
primarily treated longer reaction time as an added time cost and favored shorter-time candidate 
conditions. 
Next, without changing the BO algorithm, reference point or acquisition-function settings, we 
additionally included the real AutoDNA long-time, high-yield observation described above and 
recomputed E[HVI] under the same scan. In this case, the E[HVI] curve showed a clear peak at a 

longer per-cycle time. This result indicates that qNEHVI itself is not intrinsically unable to select 
longer reaction times; the key factor is whether the GP surrogate has learned sufficient time-yield 
information relevant to the current experimental setting. 
These analyses indicate that the limitation of the fine-tuned BO baseline does not arise from 
qNEHVI being unable to balance yield and time, but from the GP surrogate not learning an effective 
influence of time-related variables on yield. As a result, the model could not determine whether 
extending the reaction time would improve yield enough to offset the increased time cost, leading 
qNEHVI to favor shorter-time conditions. By contrast, AutoDNA does not rely solely on fitting a 
surrogate model to encoded numerical observations, but uses LLMs to integrate literature 
knowledge, experimental context and feedback results to propose experimental improvements. 
Therefore, under the same number of wet-lab iterations, AutoDNA more effectively identified key 
optimization directions, including longer reaction time, and achieved higher optimization efficiency 
in the enzymatic DNA synthesis task.

S5.4 Discussion on alternative optimization strategies 
S5.4.1 Simultaneous multi-objective planning 

The current implementation of AutoDNA optimizes one objective in each iteration (either 
time or yield in the enzymatic synthesis experiment). However, it can be extended to support 
simultaneous optimization of multiple objectives with a simple modification: the Hypothesis 
Agent can be modified to propose hypotheses targeting multiple optimization objectives 
within the same iteration. 
After this modification, we asked the Hypothesis Agent to generate 10 groups of hypotheses 
based on the yield/time feedback from the first round reported in the main text of AutoDNA 
(Supplementary Table S22). Among these, 5 groups contained hypotheses targeting different 
optimization objectives, indicating that AutoDNA has the potential to be extended to 
simultaneous multi-objective optimization. However, based on the optimization experience 
accumulated on the AutoDNA platform, the expected effects of these hypotheses may 
sometimes point in opposite directions. For example, in group 4, shortening the magnetic 
separation time was expected to reduce execution time and improve yield, whereas increasing 
the deprotection time was expected to increase execution time and reduce yield. In such cases, 
the final experimental outcome may make it difficult to distinguish the independent 
contribution of each hypothesis, thereby affecting the model’s judgement of future optimization 
directions. Therefore, in complex wet-lab tasks, the benefit of simultaneous multi-objective 
optimization should be evaluated together with the attribution difficulty of experimental 
feedback and the optimization efficiency. In future work, we will further evaluate the 
performance of different optimization strategies across different experimental scenarios.

S5.4.2 LLM-guided Bayesian optimization strategies 
Recent parallel studies have also attempted to combine LLMs with BO20,21, using the natural 
language understanding, reasoning and generation capabilities of LLMs to extend BO in candidate 
generation, constraint expression and sampling decisions for complex optimization tasks. Although 
these methods still require users to formulate the problem in advance as a BO-compatible 
optimization task, including defining search variables, parameter bounds, objective functions and 
feedback formats, their advantage lies in introducing the LLM’s understanding of natural language 
constraints, experimental background and historical feedback into the BO process, thereby 
improving candidate generation and optimization efficiency within a formalized search space. 
Such approaches could be integrated into AutoDNA as an optimization module callable by the 
Hypothesis Agent. Given a natural-language experimental objective, the Hypothesis Agent could 
use knowledge retrieved by the Literature Agent and prior experimental feedback to identify 
candidate optimization variables and formulate a BO-compatible search space, including parameter 
bounds, objectives and constraints. The Hypothesis Agent could then invoke the LLM-guided BO 
module to select experimental candidates by balancing exploration and exploitation within this 
space. The resulting protocols would be transformed and executed by the Code and Hardware 
Agents, and the wet-lab measurements would be returned to the Hypothesis Agent, which could use 

them to update the BO model and determine whether to retain or revise the search space for 
subsequent iterations. In future work, we will integrate this hybrid strategy into AutoDNA and 
further evaluate it alongside other optimization strategies across various experimental scenarios. 
S6 Multi-request serving 
S6.1 Multiple-protocol scenarios 
S6.1.1 Workflow 
In the multi-protocol scenario, AutoDNA simultaneously generates multiple enzymatic synthesis 
protocols and submits them to the Hardware Agent for parallel execution. For this scheduling 
experiment, the magnetic-separation time was set to 1 min in all four protocols. These protocols are 
as follows: 
1. Using lysis buffer as the wash buffer (based on the buffer condition evaluated in iteration 1 of 
Fig. 3); 
2. Using B&W buffer as the wash buffer (based on the buffer condition evaluated in iteration 1 of 
Fig. 3); 
3. Using B&W buffer supplemented with Tween-20 and increasing the CoCl₂ concentration to 0.5 
mM (based on the conditions evaluated in iteration 4 of Fig. 3); 
4. Increasing the nucleotide concentration to 0.5 mM (based on the condition evaluated in iteration 
5 of Fig. 3). 
According to the algorithm described in Supplementary Notes 2.7.1, the runtime scheduler 
performed merging of the four protocols (Supplementary Fig. 20). Since the first two protocols 
differed only in the wash buffer (determined by parameters in the pipetting operations during the 
washing step), all operations other than those involving the wash buffer were successfully merged. 
Subsequently, because Protocol 3 altered the concentration of CoCl₂, resulting in changes to the 
volumes of both CoCl₂ and water in the system, the corresponding instructions were not merged. 
Similarly, Protocol 4 modified the volumes of nucleotides and water, leading to the exclusion of 
related instructions. All remaining instructions were merged, ultimately consolidating the four 
protocols into a single, more efficient protocol. 

 
Supplementary Fig. 20 | Workflow of the enzymatic synthesis protocol merging process. 

 
S6.1.2 Execution time improvement 
Compared to executing the four protocols sequentially, the statically merged protocol substantially 
improved experimental efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 7). Without merging, the original four 
protocols collectively involved 1,964 atomic service calls, primarily including tube transfer, 
reagent transfer, incubation on magnetic stands, and thermal shaking. After merging, the number 
of required calls was reduced to 449. Across two independent experiments, the mean total 
execution time decreased from 1,433.7 min to 454.0 min, corresponding to a 3.2-fold speedup.

S6.3 Discussions on Consistency 
S6.3.1 Alternative Instrument Usage 
The current scheduling design is a tradeoff between consistency and instrument efficiency. To 
avoid inconsistent results, AutoDNA determines to use an alternative instrument only when it has 
identical functions as the original one (e.g., isothermal incubation). We have validated the 
consistency with an enzyme digestion experiment followed by fluorescence detection 
(Supplementary Fig. 22a).  
In this experiment, DNA oligonucleotides (USER-DNA, 10 µM, sequence in Supplementary Table 
S25) were annealed in a thermal cycler using the following program: 95 °C for 5 min, slow 
cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C per 6 s, and holding at 4 °C. Subsequently, the annealed 
USER-DNA was diluted to a final concentration of 0.2 µM in TE buffer supplemented with 12.5 
mM MgCl2. USER enzyme was added to the mixture to a final concentration of 0.02 U µL-1 and 
mixed thoroughly by pipetting. The digestion reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in a 
thermal cycler or a heater, respectively. Upon completion of the reaction, fluorescence intensity 
was measured using a fluorometer. The experiment was independently repeated three times, with 
three technical replicates per condition in each experiment. Supplementary Fig. 22b shows that the 
measured fluorescence values were not significantly different for PCR and heater. For users with 
stricter consistency requirements, AutoDNA can also be configured to disable using alternative 
instruments.

S6.3.2 Uninterruptible Experiments 
To evaluate the impact of adding uninterruptible support, we designed a scenario in which two 
fluorescence-based strand displacement reactions were performed concurrently. The experimental 
details are consistent with the siRNA section (Supplementary Notes 4). The reporter complex 
consisted of a single-stranded fluorophore and a quencher at a 1:1.5 molar ratio (1× = 10 µM). 
Annealing was performed in a thermal cycler under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, 
slow cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C per 6 s, and holding at 4 °C. A 5-nt toehold domain was 
incorporated to ensure specificity and reaction kinetics. Dynamic fluorescence measurements were 
carried out using a fluorometer of AutoDNA and 8-strip tube. All components except the input 
strand were mixed in TE buffer containing 12.5 mM MgCl₂ at stoichiometric ratios, with the 
reporter at a final concentration of 250 nM. The mixture was aliquoted into an 8-strip tube, and the 
input strand (100 nM and 150 nM DNA) was added, followed by mixing via pipetting up and 
down. The 8-strip tube was transferred to the fluorometer, and fluorescence readings began. 
Fluorescence signals were recorded at 30-second intervals. 
To verify the impact of the "uninterruptible" option on experiment scheduling, we designed two 
groups of comparative experiments. Each group consists of two sub-experiments: Experiment 1 
(100 nM input strand concentration) and Experiment 2 (150 nM). The total duration of a single 
experiment is 31 min 35 s, and the workflow includes: pipetting and transferring the 8-tube strip to 
the fluorometer (1 min 20 s); fluorescence monitoring (30 min, with a sampling interval of 30 s); 
and transferring the strip out of the fluorometer (15 s). Experiment 1 was submitted for scheduling 

at the initial time (t = 0), while Experiment 2 was submitted 16 min 38 s later. 
With the 'uninterruptible' option enabled, the scheduler enforced a strict strategy of mutual 
exclusion for instrument resources. Upon the submission of Experiment 2, since both experiments 
were marked as uninterruptible and the fluorometer was currently occupied by Experiment 1, the 
scheduler forced Experiment 2 to wait (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Experiment 2 commenced only 
after Experiment 1 had fully concluded and released the resources, thereby ensuring the integrity 
of the observational data (Supplementary Fig. 24a). 
In the absence of the uninterruptible option, the scheduler adopted a preemptive scheduling 
strategy. Upon receiving the request for Experiment 2, the scheduler immediately initiated 
pipetting operations, causing the strand displacement reaction to trigger prematurely 
(Supplementary Fig. 23b). However, as the fluorometer was still occupied by Experiment 1, 
Experiment 2 could not enter the monitoring phase. This resulted in a 14 min 20 s gap where the 
intermediate fluorescence states of the 150 nM sample were not recorded, so the complete reaction 
curve was not captured (Supplementary Fig. 24b). The detailed information of DNA sequences is 
given in Supplementary Table S26.

S7 DNA storage 
S7.1 Agent workflow 
S7.1.1 Write experiment 
The DNA data storage system processes two types of requests: write and read. As exemplified by 

the write request in Fig. 5b, the Planner Agent first decomposes the high-level objective into four 
stages: encoding, synthesis, purification, and storage (Supplementary Table S27). The encoding 
stage was identified by the Planner Agent as a purely computational (in silico) procedure, as it 
involves no wet-lab steps. Consequently, it was assigned a pure_software flag, directing agents to 
execute it as a computational task. For this stage, the Protocol Agent generates the decoding 
method for the input text (details in Supplementary Notes 7.3), the Code Agent transforms the 
method into the code, and the Hardware Agent executes the code and outputs all 78 sequences to 
synthesize.  
For the synthesis stage, the user’s prompt did not specify a methodology. The Literature Agent 
therefore proposed two alternatives: chemical and enzymatic synthesis. Based on reagent 
unavailability for the former, AutoDNA selected the enzymatic synthesis protocol previously 
established in Supplementary Notes 5.2.1. To enable the concurrent synthesis of all encoded 
oligonucleotide strands, the multiple_input field was utilized to pass all target sequences in a 
single step (Stage 2 - Step 9). (Note: The final volume is listed as 0 µL because the DNA product 
remains immobilized on magnetic beads after the liquid reagents have been removed).  
Following enzymatic synthesis, AutoDNA autonomously identified a suitable purification method 
for the enzymatic product and sequentially generated the corresponding protocol and code. For the 
final storage stage, the Protocol Agent initially generated several potential workflows based on its 
internal knowledge. However, these were deemed non-executable due to reagent constraints. In 
response, the Planner Agent initiated a comprehensive inventory check of all available reagents 
and tasked the Protocol Agent with designing a simpler, viable storage protocol. This adaptive re-
planning enabled the successful completion of the entire write workflow.

protocols were viable due to reagent unavailability. Consequently, the Planner Agent initiated a 
"gather mode" to retrieve a comprehensive list of all available reagents. This inventory revealed 
that the necessary reagents for a third-generation nanopore sequencing workflow were present. 
Therefore, the Planner Agent proceeded to generate a new protocol and the corresponding 
executable code based on the manufacturer's manual for the MinION instrument, which was then 
successfully executed to complete the sequencing stage. 
The decoding stage was also identified as a purely in silico task by AutoDNA. Since the 
sequencing data is organized as a FASTQ file, the Code Agent first designs a bioinformatic 
analysis step, which performs a global scan using the iDNA sequence (Supplementary Table S17) 
as a prefix anchor to extract the downstream 8-nt sequences. Afterward, the Code Agent designed 
the inverse decoding algorithm based on the original encoding protocol. This algorithm was 
executed by the Hardware Agent, successfully recovering the original data, which was then 
returned to the user.

S7.3 Data Encoding 
S7.3.1 Encoding Mechanism 
After reviewing the relevant literature, agents in AutoDNA designed the following encoding 
scheme (Supplementary Fig. 25). Each DNA strand consists of 8 bases, which correspond to a 
sequence of 8 ternary digits (trits). These 8 positions are functionally partitioned as follows: 
l Index Field (Positions 1-4): These four trits constitute the index field, capable of uniquely 
identifying up to 81 different sequences (3⁴). 
l Data Field (Positions 5-7): These three trits form the data field, which encodes three data units 
per strand. 
l Validation Field (Position 8): The final position is a validation trit, enabling the detection of a 
single-base error. 
 
Supplementary Fig. 25 | The structure of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) designed by the agents. 
 
The overall encoding process is performed in four main steps: 
1. Text-to-Ternary. Each character in the input text is first converted to its binary representation. 
These binary codes are then concatenated into a single binary string, which is subsequently 
converted into a ternary (base-3) sequence. 
2. Data partitioning and indexing. The resulting 234-digit ternary sequence is partitioned into 
78 blocks of 3 digits each (234/3=78). Each block is designated as the data field for a single 
DNA strand. These blocks are then assigned sequential indices, which correspond to the index 
field of each respective strand. 
3. Generation of a validation trit. For each strand, a validation trit is generated by summing all 
the ternary digits in its data and index fields. The final trit is the remainder of this sum when 
divided by 3 (i.e., the sum modulo 3). 

4. Ternary-to-DNA. Finally, the complete 8-digit ternary sequence for each strand (index + data 
+ validation trit) is converted into an 8-base DNA sequence using a rotate mapping strategy. 
This method dynamically adapts the mapping rule based on the previously synthesized base, a 
design choice that prevents the formation of homopolymers (i.e., two consecutive identical 
bases) (Supplementary Fig. 26). 
The sequences generated by the encoding mechanism above are shown in Supplementary Table 
S30. The decoding mechanism is the reverse process of encoding.

S7.3.2 Encoding Implementation 
The code for encoding is shown in Supplementary Table S31.  
Supplementary Table S31 | The generated code for DNA encoding. 
import math 
 

# --- Helper function for base conversion --- 
def int_to_base(n, base): 
    """Converts a non-negative integer to its string representation in a given 
base.""" 
    if n == 0: 
        return '0' 
    digits = [] 
    while n > 0: 
        digits.append(str(n % base)) 
        n //= base 
    return "".join(reversed(digits)) 
 
# --- Input and Constants as per the scheme --- 
SENTENCE = "Intelligence is the ability to adapt to change" 
 
DNA_MAP = { 
    'A': {'0': 'T', '1': 'C', '2': 'G'}, 
    'T': {'0': 'A', '1': 'C', '2': 'G'}, 
    'C': {'0': 'A', '1': 'T', '2': 'G'}, 
    'G': {'0': 'A', '1': 'T', '2': 'C'} 
} 
 
INDEX_TRIT_COUNT = 4 
DATA_TRIT_COUNT = 3 
 
# --- Procedure --- 
 
# Part 1: Data Preparation (Text -> Binary -> Ternary) 
 
# 1. Convert Sentence to a Single Binary Stream 
binary_stream = "" 
for char in SENTENCE: 
    # Get ASCII value and convert to 8-bit binary representation 
    ascii_val = ord(char) 
    binary_char = format(ascii_val, '08b') 
    binary_stream += binary_char 
 
# 2. Convert Binary Stream to Ternary Stream 
# Treat the entire binary stream as one large number 
large_integer_from_binary = int(binary_stream, 2) 

# Convert this large integer to its base-3 (ternary) representation 
ternary_data_stream = int_to_base(large_integer_from_binary, 3) 
 
# Part 2: Segmentation and Structuring 
 
# 3. Pad the Ternary Data Stream 
# Calculate the number of strands needed 
num_strands = math.ceil(len(ternary_data_stream) / DATA_TRIT_COUNT) 
# Calculate the total length required for the data trits after padding 
padded_length = num_strands * DATA_TRIT_COUNT 
# Add leading '0's until the stream reaches the required length 
padded_ternary_data_stream = ternary_data_stream.zfill(padded_length) 
 
# 4. Generate Source Trit Segments 
source_segments = [] 
for i in range(num_strands): 
    # i. Get Data Chunk 
    start_index = i * DATA_TRIT_COUNT 
    end_index = start_index + DATA_TRIT_COUNT 
    data_chunk = padded_ternary_data_stream[start_index:end_index] 
 
    # ii. Get Index Trits 
    index_in_ternary = int_to_base(i, 3) 
    index_trits = index_in_ternary.zfill(INDEX_TRIT_COUNT) 
 
    # iii. Combine Index and Data to form a 7-trit source segment 
    source_segment = index_trits + data_chunk 
     
    # iv. Add the segment to the list 
    source_segments.append(source_segment) 
 
# Part 3: Error Correction and DNA Conversion 
 
# 5. Generate Final DNA Strands 
final_dna_strands = [] 
for source_segment in source_segments: 
    # i. Calculate Error Trit 
    sum_of_trits = sum(int(trit) for trit in source_segment) 
    error_trit = str(sum_of_trits % 3) 
 

    # ii. Form 8-Trit Strand 
    eight_trit_strand = source_segment + error_trit 
 
    # iii. Convert to DNA 
    previous_base = 'G'  # Initial base as per the rules 
    dna_strand = "" 
    for trit in eight_trit_strand: 
        current_base = DNA_MAP[previous_base][trit] 
        dna_strand += current_base 
        previous_base = current_base 
         
    # iv. Add the completed DNA strand to the final list 
    final_dna_strands.append(dna_strand) 
 
# --- Output --- 
# Print the final list of 8-base-long DNA strings 
print("Encoded DNA Strands:") 
for i, strand in enumerate(final_dna_strands): 
    print(f"Strand {i:02d}: {strand}")

S8.2 Generated Code for Bioinformatic Analysis

autonomously generate a complete identification pipeline with multiple bioinformatic analysis 
tools (Supplementary Table S35). The process begins by indexing the reference genome (reverse-
complemented hg38). To ensure data purity, the chopper tool removes low-quality reads with an 
average quality score below 10 from the raw sequencing data. minimap2 then aligns these cleaned 
sequences to the reference genome, while samtools performs format conversion, sorting, and 
indexing to produce sorted binary BAM files. Subsequently, the program identifies potential gene 
mutations by analyzing base pileups via the mpileup and call commands in bcftools. The system 
further selects high-confidence sites by filtering for quality scores above 30 and coverage depths

S9 Supplementary Discussions 
S9.1 Contributions 
This work addresses the fundamental challenge of extending autonomous experimentation to the 
complex biological domain, moving it toward a future of intelligent, general-purpose, and 
collaborative experimentation. 
First, we introduce a fundamental shift from human-centric orchestration to AI-native autonomy. 
Prior systems require extensive human input for procedure definition, protocol optimization, and 
instrument configuration. For example, CoScientist22 only shows a single-objective optimization 
and only supports one single instrument; Virtual Lab23 requires human interventions for design 
workflow and result validation. Unlike those systems, AutoDNA’s co-design of models, hardware, 
and experiments enables end-to-end autonomy for biological experiments. This paradigm allows 
the system to handle multi-objective optimization with open dimensions, concurrent multi-user 
experiments, and complex protocols, while human intervention is limited to hardware maintenance 
tasks as described in Supplementary Notes 1.4. 
Second, we build a proven and scalable framework for complex biological workflows, which is 
essential and urgent in driving the paradigm shift of biotechnology from observational discovery 
towards engineering design. We successfully extend autonomous laboratories to the high-stakes 
domain of biomolecular research, which presents unique challenges—multi-objective experiments 
with open dimensions, heterogeneous instruments, and stringent robustness requirements—that 
existing platforms designed for chemistry and materials cannot meet. The capability of the 
framework is demonstrated through a series of end-to-end applications including nucleic acid 
synthesis, DNA data storage, and cancer gene mutation detection.  
Third, we deliver a reusable foundational infrastructure that connects multi-agent reasoning with 
physical laboratory execution. The key multi-agent contribution of AutoDNA is not a new general-
purpose planning algorithm, but the co-design of agent roles, hardware abstractions, and runtime 
execution mechanisms for complex wet-lab experiments. Specifically, AutoDNA introduces 
hardware-facing Code and Hardware Agents, together with an AI-optimized hardware abstraction 
layer that encapsulates heterogeneous instrument capabilities as "atomic services". These services 
make laboratory operations interpretable and callable by LLM agents, while code validation, error 
detection and recovery, and runtime scheduling support robust execution for long-running 
experiments. Empirically, the system demonstrates its practical value through expert-level 
autonomous DNA synthesis, a tripling of experimental throughput via intelligent scheduling, and 
the orchestration of more than 11,300 operations across 25 instruments within a DNA data storage 
application, thereby establishing new benchmarks in scale, efficiency, and reliability for automated 
biotechnology. 
In summary, our contribution is not merely a new tool but a transformation in how BTEC research 

can be conducted. We provide the architectural blueprint, the technical framework and 
infrastructure, and the empirical validation for AI-driven biological discovery (Supplementary Fig. 
31).

S9.2 Reproducibility of LLM-generated Plans 
Due to the inherent randomness in LLM inference (caused by factors such as batch processing of 
inference requests and the non-commutativity of floating-point operations), the generated 
experimental plans may vary across different runs. However, despite these differences in content, 
most plans remain executable and capable of producing the intended outcomes. Taking the 
relatively complex enzymatic synthesis workflow as an example, we repeatedly provided the same 
prompt 10 times and found that 60% of the plans were able to generate complete synthesis 
protocols that successfully produced the target sequence, albeit with variations in details. 
Supplementary Table S38 compares two of the generated plans, highlighting differences in 
temperature, duration, repetition count, and buffer composition. Nevertheless, the overall process 
(the extension–deprotection cycle) remained consistent in both cases, and both were able to 
synthesize the correct sequence.

S9.3 Error handling 
AutoDNA coordinates multiple instruments to execute long-duration wet-lab experiments, 
during which occasional hardware faults and wet-lab execution errors are unavoidable. This 
section discusses the error-handling capability of AutoDNA and its current boundaries. 
Error observability is a prerequisite for error handling in AutoDNA: the system can respond 
to an error only when a detectable signal is exposed by the relevant hardware controller, 
driver, scheduler or experimental readout. AutoDNA adopts a hierarchical error-signal 
feedback and handling mechanism. Error signals are returned through the hardware interface 
and propagated upwards along the chain of hardware device, PLC, Hardware Agent (runtime 
scheduler) and human operator, with recovery preferentially attempted at lower levels, such as 
the PLC or runtime scheduler. After AutoDNA detects an error, it mainly adopts three types 
of handling strategies: 
- Recovery at the PLC level. AutoDNA implements simple error-recovery logic in the PLC 
control layer, typically through in-place retry, such as retrying pipette-tip pickup. After 
recovery, the PLC control logic reports the error information for subsequent recording and 
analysis. 
- Recovery at the Hardware Agent level. If recovery at the PLC level is unsuccessful, the 
Hardware Agent marks the current device as “unavailable” and triggers rescheduling, 
switching the experiment to another device with equivalent functionality (Supplementary 
Notes 2.7.2). Similarly, the Hardware Agent and PLC record the corresponding error 
information. 

- Manual Recovery. If the Hardware Agent cannot complete recovery, AutoDNA pauses the 
experiment and reports the error to the operator for manual intervention. 
Across all wet-lab experiments reported in this work, AutoDNA observed 9 types of errors, 
occurring 14 times in total (Supplementary Table S39). Among these, 4 events were directly 
recovered at the PLC level, mainly through automatic recovery of pipette-related operations; 
4 events were recovered at the Hardware Agent level by migrating execution to another 
device/workstation with equivalent functionality; and the remaining 6 events required manual 
correction by the operator followed by retrying or replanning. In future work, we plan to 
introduce computer-vision monitoring and additional robotic maintenance modules to further 
reduce manual intervention during hardware fault recovery. 
For errors at the level of wet-lab results, such as contamination, AutoDNA currently relies 
mainly on agents to introduce detection mechanisms during experimental design. For example, 
negative controls and no-template controls are included in fluorescence detection experiments, 
and abnormal results are identified during subsequent analysis based on these controls. At 
present, broadly applicable hardware readouts that directly report contamination remain limited 
in wet-lab workflows, making such errors less directly observable than hardware faults.

To validate the automatic error-handling capability of AutoDNA, we designed fault-injection 
experiments and selected two representative error types from the previously reported error records 
(Supplementary Table S39): pipette tip pickup error and pipette malfunction error (e.g., liquid 
intrusion). We then manually injected these two types of faults during experiments to evaluate the 
error-recovery capability of AutoDNA. The former represents an error that can be directly 
recovered by the lower-level PLC, whereas the latter represents a system-level error that requires 
intervention by the Hardware Agent and triggers dynamic task rescheduling. 
First, we injected a pipette tip pickup error during a routine pipetting operation. Under normal 
conditions, at the start of a pipetting operation, the pipette first loads a tip from a tip box filled 
with pipette tips. In this experiment, we intentionally removed the tip from the first position of the 
tip box to simulate a missing tip caused by incomplete labware preparation by the operator. When 
the pipetting module attempted to pick up a tip from this position, its sensor detected that tip 
loading had failed and reported this exception to the lower-level PLC control system. The PLC 
control software then automatically triggered its internal retry logic, directing the pipette to move 
to other available positions to pick up a tip until the operation succeeded. This process was 
completed entirely by the lower-level PLC control logic without intervention by the Hardware 
Agent. After successful recovery, the system synchronously recorded the failed retry log and 
updated the tip inventory status (Supplementary Video 6). 
Next, we injected a pipette malfunction error in a fluorescence quantification experiment. The 
workflow consisted of four steps: adding buffer, adding sample, incubation for 2 min and reading 
concentration with the fluorometer. Under normal conditions, all steps are completed by the 
pipetting module and fluorometer on the fluorescence detection workstation. Before the second step 
(sample addition), a pipette malfunction signal was intentionally injected into the system, preventing 
the pipetting module from continuing the operation. Because the PLC could not directly recover 
from this error, it reported the error to the scheduler in the Hardware Agent. After capturing the 
error, the scheduler searched for alternative pipetting modules available in the current system, 
namely pipetting machines on other workstations, and returned the list of candidate instruments to 
the Hardware Agent. After searching, the Hardware Agent identified an idle pipette of the same type 
on the synthesis workstation. It therefore triggered dynamic task rescheduling, migrated the current 
pipetting task to the synthesis workstation and updated the scheduling decisions for subsequent steps. 
After sample addition and incubation were completed on the synthesis workstation, AutoDNA 
transferred the tube back to the fluorescence workstation because the synthesis workstation was not 
equipped with a fluorometer, and then completed the final fluorescence reading (Supplementary 
Video 6). The concentration values obtained after automatic fault-transfer recovery showed no 
significant difference from those obtained under the no-fault condition (Supplementary Fig. 32).
